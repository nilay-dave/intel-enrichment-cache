    Measurements¶

    Generated by tools/generate_measurements_doc.py from docs/metrics.json. Reproduce the whole thing with make bench.

    The stream these numbers come from¶

    Generated, not captured: a Zipf repeat distribution at alpha 1.1 over a universe 20% the length of the stream, arriving in bursts of 8 on average spread over 2 seconds, across a simulated 7 days.

    Members of a burst draw indicators independently from the Zipf distribution rather than sharing a dominant one. A real scan burst is dominated by the indicator that caused it, so this understates coalescing rather than flattering it.

    Real: what the cache and the coalescer are worth¶

    No latency model is involved in these. A request count is a property of the algorithm on a stated stream and does not depend on how long a request takes, so the experiment that produces them removes latency entirely.

    lookups days cache hit rate coalesced requests per 1,000 lookups a free tier supports
    10,000 7.0 0.544 112 444.5 1,125 a day
    100,000 7.0 0.676 190 322.5 1,550 a day

    The last column is the point of the library. VirusTotal's public tier allows 500 requests a day, so an uncached client enriches 500 indicators a day and then stops. At the measured request rate the same allowance covers about 1,550 lookups a day, which is 3.1 times as many.

    Real: what a cache lifetime policy costs¶

    policy cache hit rate requests per 1,000 lookups
    per_class_defaults 0.643 353.5
    uniform_1h 0.512 481.8
    uniform_4h 0.642 355.7
    uniform_24h 0.780 219.2
    uniform_7d 0.889 110.6

    A uniform 7d lifetime reaches a 0.889 hit rate against 0.643 for the per class defaults, at 3.2 times fewer requests. On hit rate alone the per class policy loses decisively, which is why hit rate is the wrong criterion: a longer lifetime always scores better, up to never expiring anything, which would score perfectly and be useless. What this table measures is the price of the safety, and ADR-001 is the argument about whether to pay it.

    Simulated: what hedging buys and costs¶

    The latency figures below are a simulation. There is no API behind them. They come from a lognormal body with a median of 180ms and sigma 0.45, plus 2% of responses stalling at 12 times the median, and they are only as good as that model. The request counts in the same table are real.

    policy p50 p95 p99 requests extra requests p99 change
    off 192ms 430ms 2328ms 1,490 +0.00% +0.0%
    after_400ms 191ms 432ms 598ms 1,575 +5.70% -74.3%
    after_800ms 193ms 432ms 970ms 1,518 +1.88% -58.3%
    after_1500ms 192ms 431ms 1658ms 1,514 +1.61% -28.8%

    Percentiles are nearest rank over 1,490 lookups, so the p99 is the 15th slowest observation rather than an interpolation between two that were never seen.

    The module's own docstring used to argue that hedging is expensive on a metered API. It is not: a hedge only fires on a request that has already stalled, so its cost is bounded by the 2% stall rate rather than by the traffic. Hedging after 400ms cut the simulated p99 by 74% for 5.7% more requests. The docstring now says so.

    It is still off by default. 5% more requests is cheap and it is still 5% a caller did not ask for, and the stall rate it depends on belongs to somebody else's service: a provider having a bad hour is exactly when the stall rate climbs, which is exactly when the budget can least absorb it. That is what the quota floor is for.

    What is not measured here¶

    How long a verdict stays true, which is the thing that would settle the cache lifetime defaults. No replay of an indicator stream can supply it: the stream records what was looked up, not what the answer would have been at other times. See limitations.
